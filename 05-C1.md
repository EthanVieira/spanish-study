# Level C1: Effective Operational Proficiency

**Prerequisite:** Milestone B2 reached (step B2-376 ticked). The README methods still apply; the weekly rhythm continues in the C1 form below.

**Goal at exit:** you can follow lectures, debates, satire and fast group conversation without effort; read literary prose of any period from Cervantes and Galdós to this year's debut novel, and academic and journalistic Spanish with full understanding of tone, irony and register; speak fluently and precisely on abstract and specialised topics, adjusting register at will; and write long, well-structured texts (an academic essay with sources, a 2000-word *ensayo*, a *comentario de texto*, formal *alegaciones* to a public consultation) with only occasional slips. Vocabulary about 12,000 words. You understand written Portuguese and Catalan for practical purposes, recognise the main varieties of Spanish by ear, and can read Golden Age texts in their original spelling. This is the level of the DELE C1 and of the reading a Spanish university expects.

**Time:** about 950 hours over 36 weeks, roughly 4.5 hours a day, 6 days a week, of which about an hour and a half is casual listening, free reading and television away from the desk. At 3 hours a day the level takes about 55 weeks; keep the order.

**What changes at C1** (Appendix A): the level is organised around literature and public intellectual life. Twelve books from the canon and the present, each read as a Spaniard with a *bachillerato* would read it: with the author's biography, a literary-history context and a *comentario de texto* afterwards. Language study turns to style, orthography by the RAE's rules, rhetoric, language history and neighbouring languages. Listening moves to *Hora 25*, lectures and cultural radio. Writing reaches the academic essay and the long-form *ensayo*. Grammar as such is finished; what remains is precision. Underneath the twelve books, the volume habits continue: the commute slot carries the audiobooks and one Latin American long-form episode a week, free reading moves to the living Latin American and Spanish novel (the canon above is heavy on Spain and on the dead), a second series runs from Week 26, and films go from twelve to sixteen.

## The C1 weekly rhythm

- **R1 Listening** (about 240 min): *Hoy en EL PAÍS* four episodes (or the first hour of *A vivir que son dos días* once instead of two of them), each with a 3-sentence note; *Hora 25* twice (Cadena SER's evening news analysis, Monday to Friday, 20:00 to 22:00; take the first hour on demand), each followed by a 5-sentence Spanish summary of the main interview's argument; one long programme a week in rotation (*La Cultureta*, *El ojo crítico*, *Deforme semanal*, *Saldremos mejores*, a Fundación Juan March lecture) with an 8-sentence summary; and every second week the QSD item (the book read in one sitting plus a radio episode on its theme) instead of the long programme.
- **R2 Speaking** (about 130 min): four 20-minute AI sessions in seminar mode (the assistant is a fellow student or a lecturer; topics from the week's reading; it challenges your claims and asks for evidence); one recorded talk of 8 minutes on the week's topic (Appendix F part 2), transcribed and corrected; one 3-minute reading aloud of a literary passage, compared with the audiobook where one exists; one 2-minute dictation from an *El hilo* or *Radio Ambulante* transcript or from an audiobook you own the text of, logged.
- **R3 Writing and reading** (about 200 min): a *diario de lectura* entry of 100 words on five days (what you read, one quotation, one observation about the language); one writing-bank text (Appendix I) of 400 words, corrected and filed in `writing/portfolio-C1.md`; *Babelia* and *Ctxt*: three articles a week (one a book review); El País: two articles a day, five days; Agencia SINC: two articles a week; ten harvested collocation or idiom cards.
- **R4 Poem or song** (about 40 min): one item from Appendix J, learned by heart and recited or sung on the recording for the week.

Weekly checks require all of the above plus the content steps.

**Book pattern** (C1 form): (1) before: the author's Spanish Wikipedia entry in full and the relevant LIT chapter; (2) during: audiobook chapter first where one exists; intensive on the first 20 pages, extensive after that with at most 6 lookups per 30 pages, marking in pencil every sentence whose construction you could not have produced; (3) after: a 400-word *comentario de texto* in the *bachillerato* format (*localización, tema, resumen, estructura, análisis de la forma, conclusión*) on a passage of your choice, corrected; a 6-minute AI seminar on the book; the five marked sentences copied into the notebook and imitated with your own content; and the *Un libro una hora* episode on the book where one exists (Cadena SER; it has *Cien años de soledad*, *Marianela*, *La fiesta del Chivo*, *Soldados de Salamina* and the Borges stories among others), heard in the commute slot after the *comentario* is written, never before.

**Episode pattern** (C1 form): no subtitles; one 10-minute scene rewatched with subtitles as a check only; eight sentences on the plot and two on language (an idiom, a register shift, a regional feature); 10 cards.

**Language-study pattern**: read the section named; write every rule you did not already apply into the notebook in Spanish; then apply it to three of your own portfolio texts and note what changed.

## Appendix A: What changes at C1

1. **Reading like a graduate.** Every book comes with its author's biography, its place in the literary histories, and a *comentario de texto* afterwards. That is the shared framework through which Spanish speakers with a *bachillerato* discuss literature; owning it lets you join the conversation.
2. **Language study is style.** Grammar is done. The RAE's *Libro de estilo* and *Ortografía* make your writing correct to the letter; the *bachillerato* textbook gives you the vocabulary of analysis and rhetoric; the language-history weeks let you read anything written since 1554.
3. **Listening is analysis.** *Hora 25* twice a week and a cultural programme, each summarised as an argument, not as facts. Lectures and the *¿Qué sabemos de?* books train the long attention span a university demands.
4. **Speaking is seminar.** The assistant is now a fellow student who disagrees and asks for evidence, or an examiner who asks you to nuance and relate. Register control is the skill: academic in the seminar, colloquial in the reflections, and the ability to switch mid-sentence.
5. **Writing is public and academic.** The bank moves to 400 words; the milestones demand *comentarios* and an academic essay with sources; the level ends with a 2000-word *ensayo* and *alegaciones* on a real public consultation.
6. **The neighbours.** Six weeks give you receptive Portuguese and Catalan, which every educated Spaniard has some of and which the Iberian and Latin American worlds assume. *Merlí* (or *Glória*) is the test.
7. **Varieties and history.** Andalusian, Canarian, Rioplatense, Caribbean, Andean, Mexican; the 1605 spelling of Cervantes, Larra's 1832 prose, Ortega's 1930 essay. You are not asked to produce any of it; you are asked to read and hear it without stumbling.
8. **Poems by heart.** Twenty-four poems from Manrique to Gil de Biedma, one a week, recited on tape. Nothing lodges the rhythm and the older vocabulary of Spanish more surely.
9. **The DELE C1 is the model exam.** Its four papers shape the last eight weeks, and the exit check uses its *modelo* and marking scales, with an EBAU *comentario* paper beside them.
10. **Time.** Four and a half hours a day, a third of it away from the desk (the commute slot, free reading, the series). The rhythm alone is ten hours a week. If life intervenes, the level stretches; the order does not change.

## Appendix B: C1 language checklist

1. **Sentence architecture**: control of length and rhythm; fronting for cohesion; heavy constituents last; the Spanish period (a long sentence held together by subordination) used deliberately and never by accident.
2. **Verbal vs nominal style**: nominalisation used deliberately in formal prose and avoided elsewhere; the support verbs (*llevar a cabo, proceder a, dar lugar a, poner de manifiesto, hacer hincapié en, interponer, formular*).

## Appendix E: C1 exit reading test

**Leer a Borges hoy**

Hay escritores de los que uno se cansa y escritores a los que uno llega tarde. Borges pertenece a los segundos, y no es evidente que deba ser así. La primera vez que intenté leer *Ficciones*, en el último curso del instituto, sentí que aquello era un muro: las frases sin grasa, las citas de libros que no existían, la manía de tratar la metafísica como si fuera un juego de mesa y la erudición como si fuera un chiste privado. Lo dejé a la tercera página y dictaminé, con la seguridad que solo se tiene a los diecisiete años, que era un escritor para profesores. Tuvieron que pasar veinte años y una circunstancia trivial, leer *El Aleph* en voz alta a una amiga convaleciente, para que entendiera lo que me había perdido.

Lo que en el primer encuentro parece una pose se revela, tratado con paciencia, un método. Borges no escribe así porque no sepa escribir de otra manera; escribe así porque sus cuentos tratan de personas que viven bajo un orden distinto del nuestro (el de los espejos, el de los laberintos, el de una biblioteca donde todo está escrito) y la prosa tiene que cargar con ese orden. El bibliotecario de *La biblioteca de Babel*, que dedica su vida a buscar un libro que quizá no exista, no es un loco, aunque un lector actual tienda a verlo así al principio. Obedece a una ley que él mismo no ha dictado, y el escándalo del cuento no es su obstinación, sino que tenga razón desde sus propias premisas. Es una idea difícil de sostener para quienes hemos sido educados en la convicción de que todo orden que cuesta vidas queda por ello descalificado. Borges nos obliga a mantenerla en el aire un poco más de lo que nos gustaría.

Se puede objetar que todo esto es un pasatiempo de rentista, y la objeción tiene su peso. Borges vivió de una biblioteca heredada, despreció el peronismo desde una superioridad no siempre elegante, y sus ideas sobre el destino y el coraje llevan la huella de una clase que ya no existe. Pero es una lectura pobre la que se queda ahí. Lo interesante no es que defienda un mundo antiguo, porque en rigor no lo defiende; lo interesante es que describe lo que cuesta vivir en un mundo donde el viejo orden se ha derrumbado y ninguno nuevo lo ha sustituido. Que es, a fin de cuentas, también el nuestro.

Está además la prosa, que yo tomé un día por un muro. Quien lee a Borges en voz alta, y conviene hacerlo, descubre que la sequedad de las frases no es pobreza sino ritmo. Están construidas para ser dichas, y tienen una música que la prosa actual, con sus párrafos cortos y contundentes, rara vez se permite. Hay una razón por la que sus textos se prestan tan bien a la lectura pública, y por la que él mismo, ya ciego, podía recitarlos de memoria.

Nada de esto significa que todo en Borges valga lo mismo ni que haya que leerlo sin crítica. Algunos cuentos son demasiado cerebrales, algunas ironías demasiado satisfechas de sí mismas, y la corte que se formó a su alrededor en los últimos años no le hizo ningún favor. Pero es un escritor cuyos libros mejoran a medida que uno envejece, y eso no puede decirse de muchos. Quizá deberíamos dejar de dárselo a los de diecisiete y empezar a dárselo a los de cuarenta, que ya han perdido algo y necesitan un lenguaje para la pérdida.
