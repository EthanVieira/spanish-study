# Level B2: Vantage

**Prerequisite:** Milestone B1 reached (step B1-322 ticked). The README methods still apply, and the B1 weekly rhythm continues in the heavier B2 form defined below.

**Goal at exit:** you can follow extended speech, television news and current-affairs debate, films in standard Spanish of any major variety and most natural conversation between native speakers; read contemporary literary prose, newspaper opinion pieces and official documents with ease; take part in discussion with fluency, argue a position, defend it under questioning and use the discourse markers and fillers that make speech sound Spanish; and write clear, well-structured texts of 300 to 500 words in the right register, including a formal complaint, a review, a summary and an *artículo de opinión*. Vocabulary about 8000 words. Grammar is complete and mostly automatic, including the whole subjunctive system; remaining work is register, nuance and error repair. This is the level of the DELE B2 and of most Spanish-speaking workplaces.

**Time:** about 580 hours over 34 weeks, roughly 2.5 to 3 hours a day, 6 days a week. Slightly more than half is the weekly rhythm (native podcasts, news, voice sessions, writing, songs); the rest is desk work and television. At two hours a day the level takes about 48 weeks; keep the order.

**What changes at B2** (Appendix A): everything is native material. Grammar is studied in Spanish first (*Uso de la gramática española: avanzado* and the RAE's *Nueva gramática básica*) and then in depth in English (Butt and Benjamin). *La casa de papel* and Netflix's *Cien años de soledad* replace *El ministerio del tiempo*. Literature is the centre: Laforet, García Márquez, Pérez-Reverte, Borges, Mariana Enríquez, Rulfo, Delibes, Sara Mesa, Neruda and Cortázar. Non-fiction is a popular history of Spain read alongside RTVE's *Memoria de España*, plus a daily newspaper and two long-form magazines. Speaking adds debate and news retelling. Writing reaches 300 to 500 words and the opinion column.

## The B2 weekly rhythm

Four rhythm steps every week, listed each week as R1–R4 with only the specifics; full definition here.

- **R1 Listening** (about 220 min): *Hoy en EL PAÍS* four episodes, extensive, each followed by a 3-sentence Spanish note of the argument, and once a week a 3-minute recorded oral retelling of one episode (this retelling is part of R2's count). One long-form podcast episode a week from the rotation named in the week (extensive first, then a written 8-sentence summary in Spanish, then a second listen of the 5 minutes you understood least, pausing to write down every phrase you can catch). The *Telediario* twice, no captions, five headlines checked on rtve.es/noticias.
- **R2 Speaking** (about 110 min): four AI voice sessions of 20 minutes, two of them on a set topic (the week's monologue topic in Appendix F part 2, or a debate motion from part 3 with the assistant told to argue the other side); one recorded monologue of 5 minutes on that topic, transcribed and corrected; one 2-minute dictation from an *El hilo* or *Radio Ambulante* transcript, logged in `writing/dictations.md`.
- **R3 Writing and reading** (about 150 min): diario 6 days of 10 sentences (from Week 10, three of the six entries are instead a 100-word reflection on something you read or heard); one writing-bank text (Appendix I) of 300 words, corrected and filed in `writing/portfolio-B2.md`; four newspaper articles (El País, elDiario.es, BBC Mundo, La Nación or El Universal) read with at most 6 lookups each and a two-sentence summary; five idioms or collocations harvested from the week's input into Anki.
- **R4 Song** (about 40 min): one song from Appendix J, learned by heart, every word looked up.

Weekly checks require: Anki 7/7, diario 6/7, four voice sessions, one monologue, one retelling, one dictation, one bank text, four articles, five harvested cards, one song, plus the week's content steps.

**Episode pattern** (La casa de papel, Cien años de soledad): watch with Spanish subtitles (Weeks 2–18) or without (Week 19 on), no pausing. Then one 8-minute scene three times: with subtitles, without, and pausing to shadow twelve lines. Six sentences in Spanish on the plot and one on a term of law, police, banking or, for *Cien años*, a phrase of the novel's you recognised; 10 cards. Tracker note.

**Book pattern** (B2 form): audiobook chapter first where one exists; intensive on the first 30 pages of each book (at most 12 words per chapter to the notebook), extensive after that with at most 8 lookups per 30 pages; a 250-word review and a 4-minute oral discussion with the AI ("discute este libro conmigo; pregúntame por los personajes, el final y lo que me ha parecido") after each book; the Spanish Wikipedia entry on the author read *before* starting.

**GRE pattern**: read the theme's explanation in *Uso de la gramática española: avanzado*, look up its terms in the RAE's *Nueva gramática básica* (so that you own the Spanish names: *oración subordinada, perífrasis, complemento de régimen, valor epistémico*), do the theme's exercises, check them against the key, then write 20 own sentences on the theme's points and run the Correction protocol. **BB pattern**: read the chapter, copy into the notebook every rule and example that was new to you, then write 20 sentences.

**SHADOW pattern**: one 3-minute segment a week from a *Radio Ambulante* or *El hilo* episode (both publish exact transcripts): listen twice, read, listen while reading, shadow five times, record it, compare with the original for rhythm, linking and intonation, mark the three places you were furthest off and repeat those ten times. Alternate Spain-born and Latin American voices where the episode offers them.

## Appendix A: What changes at B2

1. **No learner material.** Every source is made for native speakers except the two grammars and the DELE preparation book. When something is hard, the response is more of it, not something easier.
2. **Grammar twice.** First in Spanish with *Uso de la gramática española* and the RAE's *Nueva gramática básica*, so that you own the Spanish terms (*subordinada sustantiva, perífrasis, complemento de régimen, oración impersonal, valor de conjetura*) and can use the *Diccionario panhispánico de dudas* without translation; then the deep pass in Butt and Benjamin. After Week 30, grammar work is error analysis only.
3. **The rhythm carries the level.** Four *Hoy en EL PAÍS* a week, a long programme, two *Telediarios*, four voice sessions, a debate, a retelling, a dictation, a 300-word text, four articles, a song. That is 9 hours a week before any television or book. Do not trim it; trim the television if you must.
4. **Literature is the point.** The first Premio Nadal, García Márquez's tightest book, Spain's best-selling historical series, six of the most argued-over stories ever written, Argentina's leading living writer, the novel that made the Boom possible, the Castilian school classic, the most discussed Spanish novel of 2020, Neruda, and Cortázar. Every book is preceded by its author's Wikipedia entry and followed by a review and a discussion; that is how Spanish speakers talk about books, and you will be asked.
5. **Two spines on screen.** *La casa de papel* for Spanish crime, police and the speech of Madrid under pressure; *Cien años de soledad* for the Colombian Spanish of the novel you will read at C1. *Salvados* every week is the exam for both.
6. **Subtitles end in Week 18.** After that, subtitles are a checking tool, not a viewing aid.

## Appendix E: B2 exit reading test

**La biblioteca es más que libros**

Cuando yo era niña, la biblioteca era el único sitio del pueblo donde se podía estar sin tener que comprar nada. Una podía pasarse una hora con un tebeo, llevarse un disco, preguntarle a la bibliotecaria por lo divino y lo humano y volver a casa con una bolsa de libros por la que nadie pedía dinero. Hoy, cuando la mayoría llevamos en el bolsillo el acceso a todo el saber del mundo, se ha puesto de moda preguntar si de verdad seguimos necesitando las bibliotecas públicas. La pregunta está mal planteada. Las necesitamos más que nunca, pero por razones distintas de las que solemos dar.

Es cierto que el préstamo de libros en papel ha bajado. Las cifras del INE muestran un descenso de alrededor de un tercio desde el cambio de siglo, y los políticos partidarios de recortar señalan esa curva cada vez que hay que negociar un presupuesto. Pero los datos de préstamo cuentan solo una parte pequeña de la historia. Al mismo tiempo, el número de visitantes ha subido en muchos municipios, porque las bibliotecas han asumido funciones nuevas: son lugares de encuentro, aulas de estudio, oficinas de atención al ciudadano y, en algunos pueblos, el único sitio con un aseo público y un enchufe que cualquiera puede usar.

La función más importante, sin embargo, sigue siendo la más difícil de medir. La biblioteca es uno de los últimos lugares donde personas de orígenes muy distintos comparten un espacio sin tener que hacer nada en concreto. El jubilado que lee el periódico, la recién llegada que practica español con una voluntaria, el estudiante de bachillerato que no puede concentrarse en casa y el hombre sin hogar que solo quiere sentarse al calor. Puede que no hablen entre ellos, pero se ven, y eso no es poca cosa en una sociedad en la que, cada vez más, solo tratamos con gente que se parece a nosotros.

Los críticos objetan que esto es romanticismo y que el dinero se podría gastar mejor en colegios o en residencias de mayores. Es un dilema real, porque los presupuestos municipales están asfixiados. Pero cuando se calcula lo que cuesta una biblioteca por habitante, más o menos lo que cuesta una entrada de cine al mes, cuesta encontrar otro servicio que dé tanto por tan poco. Y los ayuntamientos que han cerrado sucursales para ahorrar suelen descubrir que el ahorro era menor de lo previsto y la pérdida para el barrio, mayor.

Hay también un argumento lingüístico que rara vez se menciona. Para los miles de personas que cada año aprenden español de adultas, la biblioteca es la escuela de idiomas más barata y mejor que existe. Hay libros de lectura fácil, audiolibros, periódicos y, sobre todo, personas con las que se puede hablar sin pagar. Es difícil imaginar una aplicación que sustituya eso.

No pretendo que la biblioteca sea la de mi infancia. Tiene que evolucionar, y ya lo está haciendo. Pero debemos dejar de medirla por cuántos libros se prestan y empezar a medirla por lo que perdería el pueblo si no estuviera. La respuesta la conocemos casi todos. Solo hace falta recordarla la próxima vez que alguien llame a la biblioteca una reliquia del pasado.

## Appendix K: Resource acquisition notes

- **Uso de la gramática española: avanzado**, Francisca Castro Viudez, Edelsa, nueva edición; 22 themes of explanation and exercises in Spanish only, made for self-study; the answer key (*claves*) is a free PDF on edelsa.es under the book's page. Buy from edelsa.es, Amazon or any Spanish bookshop. **Nueva gramática básica de la lengua española**, Real Academia Española and ASALE, Espasa, 2011, ISBN 9788467034714, 364 pages: the short, normative version of the academic grammar, in Spanish, for owning the terms. Its sister volume *Ortografía básica de la lengua española* (2012) is the reference for B2-210.
- **A New Reference Grammar of Modern Spanish**, 6th ed.: the steps name chapters by topic (word order, tenses, subjunctive, ser/estar, passive, pronouns, articles, adverbs, prepositions, conjunctions, spelling, punctuation, forms of address); use its contents and index.
- **Shadowing texts**: *Radio Ambulante* (radioambulante.org; every episode has an exact Spanish transcript) and *El hilo* (elhilo.audio; weekly, transcripts on the site).
