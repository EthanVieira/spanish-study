# Level A1: Breakthrough

**Goal at exit:** you can introduce yourself, describe your family, home, job and daily routine, handle shopping, directions, ordering and simple small talk, understand slow clear Spanish on familiar topics, read simple texts, and write short simple messages. You know roughly 800 to 1000 words, the core of Spanish grammar (articles, agreement, the present tense of regular and the main irregular verbs, *ser* vs *estar*, *gustar*, one past tense), and you pronounce Spanish well enough that a native speaker understands you first time.

**Time:** the dailies take 1 to 1.5 hours a day at this level and the steps add up to about 95 hours: about 200 hours in all. At the planned pace, over 12 weeks, that is roughly an hour and a quarter of steps a day, 6 days a week. If you only have time for the dailies and one short step a day, keep the order and let the level take 20 weeks.

**Prerequisite:** read [README.md](README.md) fully, especially the five standard methods. The step text below refers to them by name (LRS cycle, Anki rules, Recording protocol, Correction protocol, Intensive/Extensive).

## The A1 day

Every study day has the same shape: **the dailies, in the order of the table below, and then the current step**, which is the first unticked step in this file. A step is one sitting with one focus. If you finish it with time left, start the next; if it is too long for today, stop at a natural break and resume it tomorrow. Nothing else needs scheduling. There are no weekly lanes to juggle, and the week headings are only pacing marks of about six study days.

## Block 1: Sounds, alphabet, numbers (Week 1, days 3–7, and Week 2)

Spanish sound is small and regular, which is exactly why English speakers get lazy with it. Pure vowels, the tapped and trilled *r*, and the stress rules are set in this block, before any grammar, and are retested at every milestone.

- [ ] **A1-015** [Study · Deep · 45 min, one sitting] The alphabet.
      Do: Spanish has 27 letters: a–z plus *ñ* (after *n*). *ch*, *ll* and *rr* are pairs of letters, not separate letters, but you must know their sounds. On SpanishDict's pronunciation guide (spanishdict.com/guide/spanish-alphabet) listen to each letter name 3 times, repeating aloud. Search YouTube for "canción del abecedario en español" (any children's alphabet song); sing along 5 times. Write the alphabet by hand 5 times, saying each letter name aloud. Then spell aloud, letter by letter: Madrid, Zaragoza, Sevilla, Guadalajara, Ciudad de México, Buenos Aires.
      Done when: you can say all 27 letter names in order from memory in under 30 seconds, and can spell your own name and the six place names aloud with Spanish letter names.

- [ ] **A1-016** [Study · Deep · 45 min] The five vowels.
      Do: Spanish has exactly five vowel sounds, *a e i o u*, and each is always the same: short, pure, and never reduced to the English "uh". The English habits to kill are (1) turning *o* into "ow" and *e* into "ay" (Spanish *no* is not "now"; *de* is not "day"), and (2) mumbling unstressed vowels (*banana* has three identical *a*s). Watch one Butterfly Spanish or Why Not Spanish? video on the vowels. Then on SpanishDict play and repeat 5 times each: *casa, mesa, pipa, moto, luna, Panamá, elefante, Filipinas, Colorado, Honolulu*; then say 5 times each, without audio, the nonsense syllables *ala, ele, ili, olo, ulu*, keeping both vowels identical. Then diphthongs (two vowels in one syllable): *aire, seis, hoy, causa, Europa, bueno, tierra, agua, Asia, adiós, ciudad, cuidado*. Make a table in the notebook: vowel, three example words, one English trap to avoid.
      Done when: the table is complete and you have said every example word at least 5 times aloud, recording the last round and checking there is no "ay"/"ow" glide on *e* and *o*.

- [ ] **A1-017** [Study · Deep · 60 min] Consonants that behave differently from English.
      Do: on SpanishDict's pronunciation guide and one Butterfly Spanish consonant video, study these, then write the 9 rules with all 50 example words in the notebook, look up every word, play it, and say each 5 times:
      1. Single *r* between vowels is a tap (like the *tt* in American "butter"); *rr*, and *r* at the start of a word, is a trill: *pero / perro, caro / carro, pera / perra, coro / corro, rosa, Ramón*.
      2. *b* and *v* are the same sound. At the start of a phrase or after *m/n* they are like English *b*; between vowels they are soft, lips not quite closing: *beber, vivir, la vaca, Valencia, un vaso*.
      3. *d* between vowels and at the end of a word is soft, like the *th* in "the": *nada, cansado, Madrid, ciudad, todo*.
      4. *j*, and *g* before *e* or *i*, is a rough *h* from the back of the throat: *jamón, gente, jirafa, Jorge, girasol*.
      5. *c* before *e/i*, and *z*, is *th* as in "think" in Spain (*distinción*); in Latin America and the Canaries it is *s* (*seseo*): *cinco, zapato, gracias, Zaragoza, cerveza*. Use the one your variety uses, consistently.
      6. *ll* and *y* are the same sound for almost all speakers today (*yeísmo*), close to English *y* in "yes" (in Argentina and Uruguay it is "sh"): *llamo, calle, yo, pollo, ayer*.
      7. *h* is always silent: *hola, hombre, ahora, hospital, hablar*.
      8. *qu* = *k* and *gu* before *e/i* = hard *g*; the *u* is silent: *queso, quiero, guitarra, guerra*. *ñ* is like *ny* in "canyon": *año, niño, España*.
      9. *x* is *ks* in most words but *j* in *México, Texas, Oaxaca*.
      Done when: the 9 rules and 50 words are in the notebook and you have heard and said each word.

- [ ] **A1-018** [Study · Deep · 45 min] Stress and the written accent.
      Do: read GRAM's short section on stress and accentuation (or SpanishDict's guide "Spanish accent marks"). The whole system is three rules: (1) a word ending in a vowel, *n* or *s* is stressed on the second-to-last syllable: *CA-sa, HA-blan, LU-nes, co-ME-mos*; (2) a word ending in any other consonant is stressed on the last syllable: *Ma-DRID, ha-BLAR, ciu-DAD, es-pa-ÑOL*; (3) any word that breaks rule 1 or 2 carries a written accent on the stressed vowel: *ca-FÉ, MÚ-si-ca, in-GLÉS, TE-lé-fo-no, Ra-MÓN, ÁR-bol*. Accents also distinguish otherwise identical words: *tú/tu, él/el, sí/si, mí/mi, té/te, sé/se, más/mas, dé/de*, and all question words carry one (*qué, quién, dónde, cuándo, cómo, cuánto, por qué*). Write the three rules and the pairs in the notebook. Self-test: for these 20 words, mark the stressed syllable without a dictionary, then check on SpanishDict: *hospital, mesa, ventana, lápiz, joven, jóvenes, canción, canciones, examen, exámenes, difícil, reloj, ciudad, ciudades, sábado, América, universidad, papel, árbol, azúcar*.
      Done when: 18 of 20 stresses correct. If not, reread the rules and retest with 20 words of your own choice from the CS glossary.

- [ ] **A1-019** [Habit · Focused · 10 min/day] **Trill drill daily, until the sound check.**
      Do: if you can already trill, do the drill anyway to make it automatic. Watch one "how to roll your r" video (Butterfly Spanish has one). Then every day until the sound check (A1-031): 3 minutes of the "tttt-dddd" tongue-tip relaxation drill, 3 minutes on the word pairs from A1-017 rule 1, and 4 minutes reading aloud: *El perro de San Roque no tiene rabo porque Ramón Rodríguez se lo ha robado.* Record the fifth day.
      Done when: today's drill is done. The daily ends at A1-031 if the fifth-day recording has a clear difference between *pero* and *perro* and at least a two-vibration trill in *rr*. If you cannot trill at all yet, keep 5 minutes a day until the Week 6 milestone; the tap is what matters for being understood.

- [ ] **A1-020** [Input · Focused · 40 min] Pronunciation videos.
      Do: on YouTube, watch three videos from Butterfly Spanish, Why Not Spanish? or Easy Spanish about: Spanish vowels, the *r* and *rr*, and linking words in fast speech (search the channel name plus "vowels", "rr", "why Spanish sounds fast"). Write 5 practical tips you did not get from the guide into the notebook.
      Done when: 5 tips are written down.

- [ ] **A1-021** [Drill · Focused · 40 min] First 30 Anki cards: greetings and survival phrases.
      Do: create these 30 cards following the Anki rules (Spanish phrase on the front, English on the back, audio from SpanishDict or Forvo where possible): *hola · buenos días · buenas tardes · buenas noches · adiós · hasta luego · hasta mañana · gracias · muchas gracias · de nada · por favor · perdón · lo siento · sí · no · vale* (Spain; Latin American option: *está bien*) *· ¿Qué tal? · ¿Cómo estás? · Bien, gracias. ¿Y tú? · Me llamo ... · ¿Cómo te llamas? · Soy de ... · Vivo en ... · No entiendo. · ¿Puedes repetir, por favor? · ¿Hablas inglés? · Hablo un poco de español. · Mucho gusto. · ¿Cómo se dice ... en español? · Nos vemos.*
      Done when: 30 cards exist and you have done today's review.

- [ ] **A1-022** [Study · Deep · 45 min] Numbers 0–29.
      Do: *cero, uno, dos, tres, cuatro, cinco, seis, siete, ocho, nueve, diez, once, doce, trece, catorce, quince, dieciséis, diecisiete, dieciocho, diecinueve, veinte, veintiuno, veintidós, veintitrés, veinticuatro, veinticinco, veintiséis, veintisiete, veintiocho, veintinueve*. Note the accents on *dieciséis, veintidós, veintitrés, veintiséis* (rule 2 from A1-018). Note *uno* becomes *un* before a masculine noun and *una* before a feminine one: *un libro, una mesa, veintiún años*. Play each on SpanishDict. Add 30 Anki cards (front: the numeral, back: the word; add audio). Count aloud 0–29 and 29–0 ten times each. Then use a random number generator (0–29) and say 30 numbers aloud as fast as they appear.
      Done when: you can count 0–29 forwards and backwards in under 30 seconds each, and say 28 of 30 random numbers within 2 seconds.

- [ ] **A1-023** [Study · Deep · 45 min] Numbers 30–1000.
      Do: tens: *treinta, cuarenta, cincuenta, sesenta, setenta, ochenta, noventa*; from 31 up, tens and units are joined by *y*: *treinta y uno, cuarenta y cinco, noventa y nueve*. Hundreds: *cien* (exactly 100), *ciento* before anything else (*ciento dos, ciento cincuenta*), *doscientos, trescientos, cuatrocientos, quinientos, seiscientos, setecientos, ochocientos, novecientos*; hundreds agree with feminine nouns (*doscientas personas*); no *y* after the hundreds (*doscientos treinta y cuatro*). *mil* = 1000 and never takes *un*. Write out 20 numbers in words in the notebook. Add the 7 tens, *cien/ciento*, the 8 hundreds and *mil* to Anki. Watch one YouTube video on Spanish numbers. Then: random generator 30–999, say 20 numbers aloud.
      Done when: 18 of 20 random numbers said correctly within 3 seconds, and you can recite the tens 10–100 and the hundreds 100–1000 from memory.

- [ ] **A1-024** [Habit · Focused · 25 min/day] **Language Transfer daily starts: two tracks a day.**
      Do: every study day from today, the next two tracks of Language Transfer, in order, in the commute slot, until track 90 (about nine weeks).       Done when: tracks 1 and 2 are done today with the 80 percent rule met.

- [ ] **A1-025** [Input · Deep · 20 min] (1/3) Gloria Fuertes, poems 1–6.

Add 2 words per poem to Anki (12 cards).
      Done when: this sitting's share is done (about a third of the whole); stop at a natural break.

- [ ] **A1-026** [Input · Deep · 20 min] (2/3) Gloria Fuertes, poems 1–6, continued.

- [ ] **A1-027** [Input · Deep · 20 min] (3/3) Gloria Fuertes, poems 1–6, finished.

## Block 2: Core course, part 1 (Weeks 3–6)

From here on, each Complete Spanish unit is done as four steps (a, b, c, d) which are always the same:

- **(a) Dialogue**: LRS cycle on every dialogue and listening passage in the unit.
- **(b) Grammar and exercises**: read the unit's language notes; copy each rule into the notebook with the book's example plus one example of your own; do every exercise in the unit in writing; check against the key; redo every wrong item and write in the notebook *why* it was wrong.

## Appendix A: How Spanish works (primer)

1. **Family.** Spanish is a Romance language, a daughter of Latin and a sibling of Portuguese, Italian, French and Catalan. Because English borrowed half its vocabulary from Latin and French, thousands of Spanish words are recognisable on sight: *información, universidad, importante, natural, animal, hospital, problema*. Learn the regular correspondences (*-tion → -ción, -ty → -dad, -ly → -mente*) and beware the famous false friends: *embarazada* (pregnant), *constipado* (having a cold), *actualmente* (currently), *éxito* (success), *librería* (bookshop), *sensible* (sensitive).
2. **Spelling is sound.** Spanish spelling was reformed to match pronunciation. Every letter has one sound (with a few pairs: *b/v*, *c/z*, *ll/y*, *g/j*), *h* is silent, and if you can say a word you can spell it. The written accent is part of spelling, not decoration: it marks stress that breaks the rule, and it distinguishes words.
3. **Two genders.** Masculine (*el libro*) and feminine (*la mesa*). Nouns in *-o* are almost always masculine and in *-a* almost always feminine, with a short list of exceptions you learn by heart. Learn the article with every noun.
4. **Everything agrees.** Articles, adjectives and some pronouns change to match the gender and number of the noun: *las casas blancas, unos coches nuevos*. Adjectives usually follow the noun.
5. **The verb carries the person.** Each of the six persons has its own ending, so the subject pronoun is usually dropped: *hablo* already means "I speak". *Yo hablo* adds emphasis. This is why verb tables are the backbone of the whole programme.
6. **Two verbs "to be".** *Ser* for what something is; *estar* for where it is and how it is right now. *Está aburrido* (bored) vs *es aburrido* (boring). A third, *hay*, means "there is / there are".
7. **Questions and word order.** No auxiliary "do", no compulsory inversion. *¿Hablas español?* is a question because of the intonation and the marks. Word order is flexible for emphasis, but adjectives follow nouns and object pronouns precede conjugated verbs (*te quiero*).
8. **Negation is simple and double.** *No* before the verb. *Nada, nadie, nunca* after the verb keep the *no*: *No tengo nada* is correct, not "I don't have nothing" but "I have nothing".
9. **Two pasts, chosen by meaning.** Spanish distinguishes a completed event (*comí*, *he comido*) from a background state or habit (*comía*). At A1 you learn the perfect (Spain) or the simple past (Latin America) for "today" and "this week"; the imperfect comes at A2.
10. **Sound is small, speech is fast.** Five pure vowels, no reduction, no schwa, syllable-timed rhythm. Consonants are soft. Words link together (*los otros amigos* sounds like one word). The tapped *r* and trilled *rr* are the only sounds English speakers regularly fail to produce.
11. **Numbers are regular.** After 15, numbers build predictably (*dieciséis, veintiuno, treinta y uno*). *Cien* is 100 alone, *ciento* with something after it; hundreds agree in gender; *mil* is never plural when counting. The decimal separator is a comma and the thousands separator a point: *1.500,50 €*.
12. **Politeness has a grammar.** *Tú* for friends, family, children and anyone your age in Spain; *usted* for strangers who are older or in authority, and much more widely in Latin America. Spain uses *vosotros* for "you all", Latin America *ustedes*. *Por favor* and *gracias* are used less than in English; the diminutive *-ito/-ita* (*un momentito, un cafecito*) does much of the softening instead.

## Appendix B: A1 grammar checklist

By the end of A1 you must be able to produce all of the following correctly and without hesitation.

1. **Noun gender and articles**: *el libro / la mesa*; *un / una*; the exceptions *el día, el mapa, el problema, el idioma, la mano, la foto, la moto, la radio*; *el agua*; *-ción / -dad* feminine.
2. **Plurals**: *-s* after a vowel, *-es* after a consonant (*ciudad / ciudades*), *-z → -ces* (*vez / veces*), *el lunes / los lunes*; *los* and *las*, *unos* and *unas*.
3. **Adjective agreement and position**: *un coche rojo, una casa roja, coches rojos, casas rojas*; *grande, azul, fácil* invariable for gender; nationalities *español / española*; *buen, gran* before a singular noun.
4. **Subject pronouns**: *yo, tú, él, ella, usted, nosotros/as, vosotros/as, ellos, ellas, ustedes*; when to use and when to drop them; *tú* vs *usted*.
5. **Ser, estar and hay**: *ser* for identity, origin, profession, nationality, description, time; *estar* for location, state, feeling; *hay* for existence.
6. **Present tense, regular**: *hablo, hablas, habla, hablamos, habláis, hablan; como ...; vivo ...*.
7. **Irregular verbs**: *ser, estar, ir, tener, hacer, salir, poner, venir, decir, saber, ver, dar, conocer* (at least the *yo* form and the pattern).
8. **Stem-changing verbs**: *e → ie* (*querer, empezar, pensar, preferir, entender*), *o → ue* (*poder, dormir, volver, costar*), *u → ue* (*jugar*), *e → i* (*pedir, servir, repetir*).
9. **Reflexive verbs**: *levantarse, ducharse, vestirse, acostarse, llamarse*; pronoun before the conjugated verb or attached to the infinitive.
10. **Gustar and family**: *me gusta / me gustan; a mí me ...; encantar, interesar, doler; también / tampoco*; *muy* vs *mucho*; *más / menos ... que, tan ... como, mejor, peor*.
11. **Possessives and demonstratives**: *mi, tu, su, nuestro, vuestro* (+ plurals); *mío, tuyo, suyo*; *este, ese, aquel* (+ forms); *esto, eso, aquello*.
12. **Questions and negation**: *qué, quién(es), dónde, de dónde, adónde, cuándo, cómo, cuánto/a/os/as, cuál(es), por qué*; *¿?* and *¡!*; *no* before the verb; *nada, nadie, nunca* with double negation.
13. **Verb + infinitive**: *ir a* + infinitive for the future; *tener que, hay que, querer, poder, deber, necesitar* + infinitive; *estar* + gerund (*-ando / -iendo*).
14. **One past tense**: *pretérito perfecto* (*he hablado, has comido, ha vivido*; irregular *hecho, visto, dicho, escrito, puesto, vuelto, abierto, roto*) with *hoy, esta semana, ya, todavía no, alguna vez, nunca*. *Latin American option*: regular *pretérito indefinido* plus *fui/fue, hice, tuve, estuve*.
15. **Prepositions**: *a, de, en, con, sin, entre, sobre, desde, hasta, para, por* (fixed phrases); *al, del*; the personal *a*; place phrases *al lado de, delante de, detrás de, encima de, debajo de, dentro de, cerca de, lejos de, enfrente de*.
16. **Time and quantity**: clock time with *y / menos, cuarto, media*; days; months; dates with cardinals; ordinals 1st–10th; *hoy, ayer, mañana, por la mañana, el fin de semana, la semana que viene*; numbers 0–1000; *muy, mucho, poco, bastante, demasiado, algo, nada, alguien, nadie, algún / ningún*.
17. **Connectors and the imperative to recognise**: *y (e), o (u), pero, porque, que, cuando, si, también, tampoco, entonces, luego, después*; the common *tú* imperatives you hear constantly, *mira, oye, perdona, ven, dime, espera, toma*.

- **Children's songs**: all ten songs in this file are on YouTube and Spotify in many versions; the *Cantajuego* and *Toy Cantando* channels have most of them with on-screen lyrics. Search the title plus "letra" for the words.
- **Pocoyó**: the Spanish original is narrated by José María del Río; the official YouTube channels ("Pocoyó Español – Canal Oficial" for Spain, "Pocoyó – Español Latino" for Latin America) carry full episodes free. **Peppa Pig en Español – Canal Oficial** carries the Castilian dub; the Latin American dub is on the "Peppa Pig Latino" channel.
- **Your first reader.** *Vacaciones al sol* by Lourdes Miquel and Neus Sans, in the *Lola Lago, detective* series, Difusión, level A1 (the series' lowest level; the next titles, *Poderoso caballero* and *Una nota falsa*, are A2 and are used then). Buy from difusion.com, Klett, Amazon, or any Spanish bookshop; the audio is downloadable from the publisher. For the second reader in A1-158, or a third if the failure handling sends you back: Difusión's *Aventuras para 3* series (A1), or the *Español con Juan* A1 readers by Juan Fernández. Whatever you pick, apply this test before starting: open three random pages and count the words you know. If you know fewer than 6 in 10, the book is too hard for A1-117; pick an easier one.
- **Extra en español**: the 13-episode learner sitcom produced by Channel 4 Learning in 2002, widely uploaded to YouTube in full (search "Extra en español episodio 1"). Episodes 1–2 at A1; the rest at A2.
- **Films**: *El laberinto del fauno* (2006) and *Volver* (2006) are on the usual rental platforms (Apple TV, Amazon, Google Play) and periodically on Netflix and other subscription services; DVDs and Blu-rays with English subtitles are cheap second-hand.
- **Podcasts**: *Simple Stories in Spanish* (Small Town Spanish Teacher; short present-tense stories, transcripts at smalltownspanishteacher.com; aimed at A1–A2), *Notes in Spanish Inspired Beginners* (Ben and Marina, Madrid; aimed at A2), *Español con Juan* (Juan Fernández; aimed at A2–B1), *Coffee Break Spanish* (Radio Lingua; season 1 is 40 free lessons for absolute beginners, taught in English, and is a good optional replacement for the commute slot if you finish Language Transfer early). At A1 all but the first are a stretch only; *Simple Stories* fills the commute slot from Week 11 (A1-163).
- **Pimsleur Spanish** (Castilian and Latin American editions, five levels of 30 lessons each) is not used in this guide because Language Transfer covers the same ground free; if you prefer it, do Level 1 in the commute slot in Weeks 3–8 instead of doubling up on Language Transfer.
- **Official tests, for this and later levels**: DELE A1, A2, B1, B2, C1 and C2 are run by the Instituto Cervantes; full *modelos de examen* with audio and keys are at examenes.cervantes.es under each level. The pass mark is 60 percent in each of the two skill groups. SIELE (siele.org) is the online alternative and publishes a free practice test.
